## Domain 2 — Geography (GEO)

- IND-GEO-01 (K) — The mature Harappan world, 2600–1900 BCE, was spread over northwestern South Asia and was one of the first large-scale urban societies of the ancient world. Source: Shinde et al. 2019, *Cell* (scholarly).
- IND-GEO-02 (K) — Its large regional urban centres included Harappa in Punjab, Mohenjo-daro in Sindh, Rakhigarhi in Haryana, Dholavira in Kutch/Gujarat, and Ganweriwala in Cholistan. Source: Shinde et al. 2019, *Cell* (scholarly).
- IND-GEO-03 (K) — Rakhigarhi lies about 150 km northwest of Delhi in Hisar District, Haryana, and is one of the largest known Indus sites. Source: Shinde et al. 2019, *Cell*, and its accompanying *Cell* commentary (scholarly).
- IND-GEO-04 (K) — Harappa lies by a former course of the Ravi River in Punjab, Pakistan, about 24 km west of Sahiwal, and Mohenjo-daro lies on the west bank / Indus plain of the lower Indus in Larkana District, Sindh. Source: Harappan Sites synthesis, digitallylearn.com, summarising excavation records (reference).
- IND-GEO-05 (K) — Dholavira lies on Khadir Island in the Rann of Kutch, Gujarat, between the seasonal Mansar and Manhar streams, and is a stone-built, three-part city with large rock-cut water reservoirs. Source: Harappan Sites synthesis, digitallylearn.com; CGPSC Sites and Town Planning notes (reference).
- IND-GEO-06 (K) — Lothal lies in the Bhal region of Gujarat near the Gulf of Khambhat, on an old course associated with the Sabarmati / Bhogava system, and contains a large brick-lined basin interpreted by its excavator S. R. Rao as a dockyard, an interpretation some scholars dispute in favour of a reservoir. Source: Harappan Sites synthesis, digitallylearn.com; CGPSC Sites and Town Planning notes (reference).
- IND-GEO-07 (K) — Ganweriwala lies in the Cholistan desert in Pakistani Punjab on the dry bed of the Ghaggar-Hakra, was rediscovered in M. R. Mughal's 1970s survey, and is counted among the five great cities although it has not been properly excavated. Source: Harappan Sites synthesis, digitallylearn.com (reference).
- IND-GEO-08 (K) — The settlement system stretched from Shortughai on the Amu Darya (Oxus) in northeastern Afghanistan in the north to Daimabad in Maharashtra in the south, and from Sutkagan Dor on the Makran coast near the Iranian border in the west to Alamgirpur in western Uttar Pradesh in the east. Source: Harappan Sites synthesis, digitallylearn.com; INFLIBNET Archaeological Anthropology chapter (reference / teaching).
- IND-GEO-09 (K) — Sutkagan Dor, on the Makran coast, was the westernmost Harappan settlement and is interpreted as a maritime trading station between the Indus region and the Persian Gulf. Source: Indo-Mesopotamia relations, Wikipedia (reference).
- IND-GEO-10 (K) — The Ghaggar-Hakra is today an intermittent, monsoon-fed river: called Ghaggar before the Ottu barrage in India and Hakra downstream in the Thar Desert in Pakistan, connected to paleochannels of the Sutlej and possibly the Yamuna that once ended in the Nara, a delta channel of the Indus joining the sea via Sir Creek. Source: Ghaggar-Hakra River, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-GEO-11 (K) — A large number of mature Harappan sites (2600–1900 BCE) are found along the middle course of the now dried-up Hakra in Pakistan, and no Indus sites have been found further south than the middle of Bahawalpur District, suggesting the Hakra ended there in terminal lakes. Source: Ghaggar-Hakra River, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-GEO-12 (K) — Estimates of the mature Harappan area and site count vary by scholar and by counting rule: published syntheses give roughly 680,000 sq km (Kenoyer), about 1 million sq km (Possehl), up to about 1.25–1.5 million sq km in later syntheses, and more than 1,500–2,000 documented settlements. Source: INFLIBNET Archaeological Anthropology chapter; Harappan Sites synthesis, digitallylearn.com (reference / teaching).
- IND-GEO-13 (K) — Sea-going and river vessels were known in the Indus region: Indus seals and a Mohenjo-daro tablet depict boats, including vessels shown with land-finding birds, dated broadly 2500–1750 BCE. Source: Indo-Mesopotamia relations, Wikipedia; Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-GEO-14 (K) — Indus pottery and seals have been found along the sea routes between the Indus and Mesopotamia, notably at Ras al-Jinz at the tip of Arabia (Oman), demonstrating maritime Indus connections with Oman and the Gulf. Source: Indo-Mesopotamia relations, Wikipedia; Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).

## Domain 3 — Politics and administration (POL)

- IND-POL-01 (F) — No excavated Harappan building has been securely identified as a royal palace, a royal tomb, or a temple of the kind that proclaims a king in contemporary Egypt and Mesopotamia, and no monumental royal inscription, victory stele, or king list has been found. Source: Theories of Harappan Political Organization, IASPOINT synthesis; Jacobsen / "An ancient stateless civilization" article excerpt (reference / scholarly excerpt).
- IND-POL-02 (K) — The so-called "Priest-King" is a 17.5 cm steatite bust from Mohenjo-daro whose label is an early excavators' interpretation; whether its subject was a priest, a king, both, or neither is not established. Source: Theories of Harappan Political Organization, IASPOINT synthesis (reference).
- IND-POL-03 (K) — Large houses exist at Harappan sites, but none matches the expected scale of a palace, although some citadel buildings at Mohenjo-daro could have been a functional equivalent. Source: Political System of Harappa Civilization, LotusArise synthesis (reference).
- IND-POL-04 (K) — Harappan cities show planned layouts, in several cases a grid of streets, a citadel / raised platform and a lower town, and at Dholavira a three-part division. Source: CGPSC Sites and Town Planning notes; Theories of Harappan Political Organization, IASPOINT synthesis (reference).
- IND-POL-05 (K) — Mohenjo-daro had extensive municipal water and drainage works, including covered brick drains, house bathing platforms connected to drains, soak pits, and on the order of 700 wells, plus the waterproofed Great Bath on the citadel mound. Source: Harappan Sites synthesis, digitallylearn.com; "An ancient stateless civilization" article excerpt (reference / scholarly excerpt).
- IND-POL-06 (K) — Standardisation is civilisation-wide: the same square steatite stamp-seal form with a perforated boss on the reverse, the same undeciphered script, the same weight system, and bricks in a consistent 1:2:4 thickness:width:length ratio occur across regions more than 1,000 km apart. Source: Theories of Harappan Political Organization, IASPOINT synthesis; Harappa.com, Cubical Weights (scholarly site).
- IND-POL-07 (K) — That standardisation implies a sophisticated mechanism for coordination and decision-making, but it does not by itself prove a single central government, because key uniformities such as the brick ratio have pre-urban origins in diffused good practice. Source: Theories of Harappan Political Organization, IASPOINT synthesis; "An ancient stateless civilization" article excerpt, citing Kenoyer 1998 (reference / scholarly excerpt).
- IND-POL-08 (H) — The Harappan world was a single centralised empire ruled by priest-kings from Mohenjo-daro and Harappa (the Piggott / Wheeler model). Source: Political System of Harappa Civilization, LotusArise synthesis, presenting this as a theory (reference).
- IND-POL-09 (H) — The Harappan world was a network of independent or loosely linked city-states / regional polities, with distinct regional material cultures, and no one city serving as a capital. Source: "An ancient stateless civilization" article excerpt, citing Possehl 1998 (scholarly excerpt).
- IND-POL-10 (H) — The Harappan world had no state at all, and its large-scale public goods were organised substantially by merchants specialising in long-distance trade (the Fairservis / stateless-society model). Source: "An ancient stateless civilization" article excerpt; Political System of Harappa Civilization, LotusArise synthesis (scholarly excerpt / reference).
- IND-POL-11 (K) — Structures long called "granaries" at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa are of inferred function only: the Mohenjo-daro building yielded no grain remains and is also called a "great hall" by many archaeologists. Source: CGPSC Sites and Town Planning notes (reference).
- IND-POL-12 (K) — Clay seal impressions from Harappa bear cord or sack marks on their reverse, showing that seals were in fact used to seal bundles of merchandise. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).

## Domain 4 — People movement (MOV)

- IND-MOV-01 (K) — Shortughai (Shortugai), in Darqad District, Takhar Province, northern Afghanistan, was a Harappan trading colony on the Oxus (Amu Darya) near the lapis lazuli mines of Badakhshan, and is the northernmost Indus settlement. Source: Shortugai, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-MOV-02 (K) — Shortughai was not mere contact: it yielded square Indus seals with script and animal motifs (including a rhinoceros seal), Harappan pottery, bricks of typical Harappan measurements, carnelian and lapis beads, bronze objects, and clay cattle-and-cart models, in a settlement of about 4 hectares in two mounds. Source: Shortugai, Wikipedia, opened in full, citing Francfort's *Fouilles de Shortughai* and Singh 2008 (reference).
- IND-MOV-03 (K) — Kenoyer's stated purpose for Shortughai is procurement: the colony may have been established to obtain gold, copper, tin and lapis lazuli and other exotic goods from Central Asia. Source: Kenoyer 1998, *Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization*, quoted in Shortugai, Wikipedia, opened in full (scholarly, as quoted).
- IND-MOV-04 (K) — Mesopotamian texts attest a Meluhha settlement / "village of Meluhha" in southern Sumer near Girsu, tentatively identified with Guabba, with references concentrated in the Akkadian and especially Ur III periods and mentions of large boats there. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-MOV-05 (K) — An Akkadian cylinder seal, Louvre AO 22310, is inscribed for Shu-ilishu, "interpreter of the Meluhhan language," showing that Mesopotamia maintained a named, official interpreter for the language of Meluhha. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-MOV-06 (K) — Mesopotamian inscriptions name individual Meluhhans in Mesopotamia: Naram-Sin lists a "man of Meluhha" among rulers, Gudea of Lagash refers to Meluhhans who came to Sumer to sell gold dust and carnelian, and Rimush's inscription places Meluhhan troops in a battle in the Elam / Parahshum area c. 2200 BCE. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-MOV-07 (K) — Indus and Indus-type seals have been excavated in Mesopotamia, particularly at Ur, Babylon and Kish, but the total is small: on the order of twenty Indus seals in Mesopotamia, nine dated to the Akkadian period (2334–2154 BCE) and two to Isin-Larsa (2000–1800 BCE), and only fourteen Indus weights in total from Mesopotamia, Iran (Susa) and the Gulf, one of them from Ur. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full, quoting Andrew Robinson (reference).
- IND-MOV-08 (K) — Direct Mesopotamian trade with Meluhha appears to have subsided during the Ur III period and been replaced by trade via Dilmun, and there are no known mentions of Meluhha in Mesopotamian texts after 1760 BCE. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-MOV-09 (K) — Isotopic work cited in the Rakhigarhi literature indicates first-generation migrants were present in Harappan cemeteries such as Harappa and Farmana, so Harappan cities contained people who had moved within their lifetimes. Source: *Cell* commentary on Shinde et al. 2019, citing Shinde et al. 2018 and Valentine et al. 2015 (scholarly).
- IND-MOV-10 (H) — The Meluhha settlement in Sumer was a resident trading colony / diaspora community of Indus people, rather than a quarter used only by visiting seasonal merchants. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full, presenting the Guabba identification as tentative (reference).
- IND-MOV-11 (H) — Indus merchants took control of the lapis lazuli supply to Mesopotamia by about 2500 BCE through Shortughai, displacing the earlier overland route through Iran. Source: Protohistoric India: Trade and Exchange in the Indus Civilization, Medium synthesis (reference, weak) — retained as (H) because the monopoly claim exceeds the excavated evidence cited.

## Domain 5 — DNA and population (DNA)

- IND-DNA-01 (F) — Exactly one genome-wide ancient-DNA dataset from an individual buried at a Harappan site itself has been published: the Rakhigarhi individual I6113, a female, archaeologically dated c. 2800–2300 BCE. Source: Shinde et al. 2019, *Cell*; *Cell* commentary on Shinde et al. 2019 (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-02 (K) — Obtaining I6113 was at the limit of preservation: powder was taken from 61 skeletal samples from the Rakhigarhi cemetery about 1 km west of the town, more than 200 libraries were sequenced, and the best-preserved individual yielded 31,760 authentic SNPs. Source: *Cell* commentary on Shinde et al. 2019 (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-03 (K) — I6113 is best modelled as a mixture of ancestry related to ancient Iranians (the largest component) and ancestry related to Southeast Asian hunter-gatherers, a profile with no detectable ancestry from Steppe pastoralists or from Anatolian or Iranian farmers as such. Source: Shinde et al. 2019, *Cell*, summary and graphical abstract (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-04 (K) — The Iranian-related ancestry in I6113 derives from a lineage that split from Iranian plateau lineages more than 12,000 years ago, before the ancestors of Iranian farmers, herders and hunter-gatherers separated, so it does not record a large-scale migration of western Iranian farmers into South Asia. Source: Shinde et al. 2019, *Cell* (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-05 (K) — Eleven genetic outliers at sites in cultural contact with the Indus world share I6113's distinctive profile: 3 from the BMAC site of Gonur in Turkmenistan, radiocarbon dated 2500–2000 BCE, and 8 from Shahr-i Sokhta in eastern Iran, dated by radiocarbon or context to 3300–2000 BCE, with 11–50% ancestry related to South Asian hunter-gatherers (AASI / AHG-related) and the rest from Iranian farmer- and West Siberian hunter-gatherer-related sources, and no detectable Anatolian farmer ancestry. Source: Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Science*, author manuscript (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-06 (K) — Narasimhan et al. name that shared profile the "Indus Periphery Cline" and argue, on six circumstantial lines of evidence, that it was plausibly characteristic of the Indus civilisation's population, while stating explicitly that without DNA from IVC burials they could not make that statement definitively — a gap I6113 then partly filled. Source: Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Science*, author manuscript (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-07 (K) — In Narasimhan et al.'s formation model, based on genome-wide data from 523 ancient individuals spanning about 8,000 years, Indus Periphery-related people are the single most important source of ancestry in modern South Asians. Source: Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Science* (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-08 (K) — After the Indus civilisation's decline, Indus Periphery-related population mixed in the northwest with groups carrying Steppe pastoralist ancestry to form the "Ancestral North Indians" (ANI), and in the southeast with local groups to form the "Ancestral South Indians" (ASI); mixtures of ANI and ASI drive the main gradient of genetic variation in South Asia today. Source: Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Science* (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-09 (K) — Steppe pastoralist-derived ancestry, ultimately descended from Yamnaya-related populations, spread south into Central Asia by the turn of the 2nd millennium BCE and integrated further south through the first half of the 2nd millennium BCE, contributing up to 30% of the ancestry of some modern South Asian groups, with a profile matching Bronze Age Eastern Europe. Source: Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Science* (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-10 (K) — The main BMAC population carried no Steppe pastoralist ancestry in the earlier phase and did not contribute substantially to later South Asians; Steppe ancestry appears at BMAC sites first in outlier individuals around the turn of the 2nd millennium BCE. Source: Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Science*, author manuscript (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-11 (K) — Narasimhan et al. state that the southward spread of Steppe ancestry was likely a conduit for the spread of Indo-European languages into South Asia; this is the paper's inference linking genes to language, not a direct observation of language. Source: Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Science* (scholarly).
- IND-DNA-12 (H) — I6113 is representative of the population of the whole Indus civilisation, across its thousand-kilometre extent and seven centuries; the *Cell* commentary flags this as an assumption, noting the civilisation was cosmopolitan and its centres spread over thousands of kilometres. Source: *Cell* commentary on Shinde et al. 2019 (scholarly).

## Domain 6 — Material economy (ECO)

- IND-ECO-01 (K) — The standard Harappan weights are cubical, commonly of chert, and conform to a binary ratio series 1:2:4:8:16:32:64 at the lower denominations, with the smallest weight in the cited series at 0.856 g and the most common weight at about 13.6–13.7 g, the 16-ratio denomination. Source: Harappa.com, Cubical Weights, by / citing Jonathan Mark Kenoyer (scholarly site), opened in full.
- IND-ECO-02 (K) — At larger denominations the weight system switches to decimal increases, the largest cited weight being 100 times the 16-ratio weight. Source: Harappa.com, Cubical Weights (scholarly site), opened in full.
- IND-ECO-03 (K) — Harappa.com, following Kenoyer, states these weights were used in all settlements and may have been used for controlling trade and possibly for collecting taxes; the tax use is an inference, not a read text. Source: Harappa.com, Cubical Weights (scholarly site), opened in full.
- IND-ECO-04 (K) — Mesopotamian texts and the *Enki and Ninhursag* tradition list Meluhhan exports as including carnelian, lapis lazuli, gold (including gold dust), timber and precious woods (named *mes* and *aba* wood), and ivory. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference, reporting cuneiform sources).
- IND-ECO-05 (K) — Etched carnelian beads in the Royal Cemetery of Ur, First Dynasty of Ur (2600–2500 BCE), were probably imported from the Indus Valley, and long etched carnelian beads are a recognised Harappan craft product. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-ECO-06 (K) — Goods proposed in the literature as sent from the Indus / Meluhha to Mesopotamia in exchange for silver, tin, woollen textiles, and possibly oil, grain and other foods include timber, ivory, lapis, gold, carnelian and glazed stone beads, Gulf pearls, shell and bone inlay, and possibly cotton textiles and chickens; the list beyond the textually named items is explicitly "less sure." Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full, summarising Possehl's "Middle Asian Interaction Sphere" (reference).
- IND-ECO-07 (K) — Craft production was specialised and sometimes concentrated: Chanhudaro, a town of under 7 hectares in Sindh, was almost wholly given to crafts including carnelian bead-making, shell working, metal working, and seal and weight making, and Lothal had a bead factory and a warehouse with sealings. Source: Harappan Sites synthesis, digitallylearn.com (reference).
- IND-ECO-08 (K) — Staple foods varied by region and period and included wheat and barley as the main staples in the Indus core, with lentils and other pulses (peas, chickpeas, green gram, black gram), sesame for oil, and, particularly in Gujarat, native millets, with African millets present by about 2000 BCE if not before. Source: Harappa.com, "What kinds of things did the Indus people eat?", citing Fuller 2002/2003 and Weber & Belcher (scholarly site).
- IND-ECO-09 (K) — Harappans fed local wild rice to animals and probably began to cultivate rice, but rice did not become an important crop until post-Harappan times; claims for major mature-Harappan rice cultivation exceed this evidence. Source: Harappa.com, "What kinds of things did the Indus people eat?" (scholarly site).
- IND-ECO-10 (K) — Both winter (rabi: wheat, barley, pea, lentil, chickpea, linseed, mustard) and summer (kharif: rice, millets, green/black gram, horse gram, sesame, cotton) crops formed part of Harappan agricultural systems, i.e. double cropping was practised across the region. Source: Pokharia et al. / archaeobotany of millets review, *Journal of Archaeological Science* via ScienceDirect (scholarly).
- IND-ECO-11 (K) — Cotton was a Harappan product: it appears in the summer crop package, and cotton textiles are among the goods proposed as Indus exports not native to Mesopotamia. Source: archaeobotany of millets review, ScienceDirect; Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (scholarly / reference).
- IND-ECO-12 (K) — At Kanmer in Kachchh, Gujarat, archaeobotany shows a shift over time toward summer drought-resistant crops in the Late phase (c. 2000–1700 BCE), a strategy the excavators link to the prevailing ecological conditions. Source: Pokharia et al. 2013, *Radiocarbon*, "Subsistence System, Paleoecology, and 14C Chronology at Kanmer" (scholarly).
- IND-ECO-13 (F) — No coinage has been found in Harappan contexts, and no readable Harappan account, price list, or tax record survives, because the script is undeciphered and the surviving inscriptions average about 4 signs. Source: Shortugai, Wikipedia, opened in full (absence of coinage); Glossa-Lab Gap Analysis 2026-11-25 (program record: Holdat corpus 1,670 seals, 7,002 tokens, mean inscription about 4 signs).

## Domain 7 — Environment and chronology (ENV)

- IND-ENV-01 (K) — The conventional phasing is Early Harappan c. 3300–2600 BCE, Mature Harappan c. 2600–1900 BCE, and Late Harappan c. 1900–1300 BCE, with de-urbanisation, loss of script, seals and standard weights, and dispersal into smaller settlements after about 1900 BCE rather than a sudden destruction. Source: Harappan Sites / IVC syntheses, digitallylearn.com and CRV Science wiki (reference).
- IND-ENV-02 (K) — Rakhigarhi's occupation relevant to I6113 is dated by seven charcoal dates with point estimates of 2800–2300 BCE, largely within the mature phase, and I6113 herself is archaeologically dated c. 2800–2300 BCE. Source: Shinde et al. 2019, *Cell* (scholarly).
- IND-ENV-03 (K) — A paleolake record at Kotla Dahar, Haryana, adjacent to Indus settlements, shows an abrupt about 4 per mille rise in gastropod oxygen isotopes at about 4.1 ka (c. 2100 BCE), marking a weakening of the Indian summer monsoon, within the radiocarbon range for the beginning of Indus de-urbanisation. Source: Dixit et al. 2014, *Geology* 42:339–342 (scholarly).
- IND-ENV-04 (K) — The "4.2-kiloyear event" is a widespread aridification at about 4.2 ka also linked, debatably, to the fall of Egypt's Old Kingdom and of the Akkadian Empire; specialists note the event was not a uniform global drought and did not happen on a single clear timeline, so its causal role for the Indus is debated, not settled. Source: Dixit et al. 2014, *Geology*; 4.2-kiloyear event, Wikipedia (scholarly / reference).
- IND-ENV-05 (K) — A Tso Moriri Lake record from the northwestern Himalaya indicates a long cold, arid interval of weakened monsoon from about 4350 to 3450 cal yr BP, contemporaneous with the displacement and de-urbanisation of the Indus civilisation, which its authors attribute to enhanced El Niño intensity and frequency. Source: Tso Moriri arid interlude paper, *Quaternary International* via ScienceDirect (scholarly).
- IND-ENV-06 (K) — In the Ghaggar-Hakra system, the Sutlej (and, on older accounts, the Yamuna) diverted away well before mature Harappan times — Sutlej abandonment of the Ghaggar course beginning about 15,000 years ago and complete by about 8,000 years ago (Singh et al. 2017; Clift et al. 2012 zircon dating places Himalayan flow in those channels between 47,000 and 10,000 BCE) — leaving a monsoon-fed, seasonal river in Harappan times. Source: Ghaggar-Hakra River, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference, reporting Clift et al. 2012 and Singh et al. 2017).
- IND-ENV-07 (H) — The Ghaggar was nonetheless perennial in the Harappan heartland during 9–4.5 ka through reactivation by Sutlej distributaries, facilitating early Harappan settlement, and declined at the start of the Meghalayan Stage — a claim its authors call "unequivocal" but which sits in tension with IND-ENV-06 and remains contested. Source: Chatterjee et al. 2019, *Scientific Reports*, "On the existence of a perennial river in the Harappan heartland" (scholarly).
- IND-ENV-08 (K) — Optically stimulated luminescence dating on the Ghaggar-Hakra floodplain (Durcan, Thomas, Gupta, Pawar, Singh & Petrie) finds fluvial deposition mostly pre-dating 5 ka, with later fluvial deposits at 3.8 ka and 3.0 ka post-dating the decline of urbanism, and concludes there is no clear link to a 4.2 ka abrupt event and no simple fluvial-to-aeolian switch: the system was highly dynamic, and the decline of urbanism was neither spatially nor temporally instantaneous. Source: Durcan et al. 2017, *Journal of Asian Earth Sciences* / ResearchGate full text (scholarly).
- IND-ENV-09 (K) — Indus populations occupied urban settlements on the Ghaggar-Hakra plains from about 4.5 ka until abandonment by about 3.9 ka, against a backdrop of a weakening monsoon, and after about 4 ka settlement displaced eastward toward the Yamuna and Sutlej headwaters and toward Gujarat. Source: Durcan et al. 2017 (Petrie co-author), full text (scholarly).
- IND-ENV-10 (K) — Settlement-survey contemporaneity is a known trap: surface-survey sites cannot be assumed to have all been occupied throughout a period, and Petrie & Lynam's re-analysis shows settlement systems varied considerably between regions of the Indus world, which matters for any population or political inference drawn from site counts. Source: Petrie & Lynam 2019, *Journal of Field Archaeology* (scholarly).
- IND-ENV-11 (K) — The older "Aryan invasion destroyed the cities" explanation is rejected in current syntheses: de-urbanisation from about 1900 BCE precedes the Steppe-ancestry integration documented genetically for the first half of the 2nd millennium BCE, and the favoured explanations are multi-causal, led by monsoon weakening and river change, with loss of Mesopotamian trade contributory. Source: Decline of the Indus Valley Civilisation synthesis, digitallylearn.com; Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Science* (reference / scholarly).

## Domain 8 — Language contact (CON)

- IND-CON-01 (K) — *Meluhha* (Sumerian *Me-luh-ha*, with KI "country") is the Sumerian name of a prominent trading partner of Sumer; most scholars associate it with the Indus Valley Civilisation, but the identification is stated in the literature as an open question, not a proof. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-CON-02 (K) — Sumerian texts repeatedly name three overseas trading centres in outward order: Dilmun, now identified with Bahrain, Failaka Island and the adjacent eastern Arabian coast; Magan, now identified with the UAE and Oman; and Meluhha, the furthest. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-CON-03 (K) — Sargon of Akkad (2334–2279 BCE) boasted that ships from Meluhha, Magan and Dilmun made fast at the docks / quay of Akkad, a direct cuneiform attestation of Meluhhan ships reaching Mesopotamia. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full; Indo-Mesopotamia relations, Wikipedia (reference).
- IND-CON-04 (K) — The Shu-ilishu seal (see IND-MOV-05) attests a distinct "Meluhhan language" that Mesopotamian administration treated as needing a professional interpreter; it gives no specimen of that language. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-CON-05 (F) — No bilingual Indus–known-language text exists, no confirmed language for the Indus script exists, and the program's audited register holds exactly one independently sourced anchor (M047) out of a strict core of 94 candidate anchors. Source: Glossa-Lab Gap Analysis 2026-11-25 (program record).
- IND-CON-06 (K) — Vedic Sanskrit contains a substantial body of non-Indo-European vocabulary: Kuiper identified 383 specifically Rigvedic non-Indo-Aryan words (about 4% of its vocabulary), Oberlies counts 344–358 "secure" non-Indo-European words, and even after removing personal and place names some 211–250 foreign words (about 2%) remain. Source: Substratum in Vedic Sanskrit, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference, reporting Kuiper and Oberlies).
- IND-CON-07 (K) — Those Rigvedic loanwords reflect village life — local flora and fauna, agriculture, artisanship, clothing, household items, dance and music, some religion — and not the intricate civilisation of the Indus cities, befitting a post-Harappan timeframe. Source: Substratum in Vedic Sanskrit, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-CON-08 (K) — Only an estimated 30–40 Dravidian loanwords are claimed in Vedic, and Witzel finds Dravidian loans only from the middle Rigvedic period onward, suggesting Indo-Aryan contact with Dravidian speakers came after the earliest Rigvedic phase in the Punjab. Source: Substratum in Vedic Sanskrit, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference, reporting Zvelebil and Witzel).
- IND-CON-09 (H) — A prefixing language related to but not identical with Munda — Witzel's "Para-Munda," later renamed the Kubha-Vipas substrate — was the language of the northern Indus civilisation and the source of the earliest Rigvedic substrate layer, identified by recurring prefixes such as *ka-* / *ki-*. Source: Substratum in Vedic Sanskrit, Wikipedia, opened in full, reporting Witzel and Kuiper (reference).
- IND-CON-10 (K) — The Para-Munda proposal is contested on method: Krishnamurti deems the evidence too meagre and faults Witzel for not showing a large number of complete, unanalysed Munda words borrowed into the first phase of the Rigveda. Source: Substratum in Vedic Sanskrit, Wikipedia, opened in full, reporting Krishnamurti (reference).
- IND-CON-11 (K) — Retroflexion in Indo-Aryan is an areal feature of the subcontinent (present in Dravidian, Munda, Burushaski and Nuristani, absent from Proto-Indo-European and Proto-Indo-Iranian), so a retroflex-bearing substrate in the northwest is likely, but retroflexion alone does not identify the donor language, and Hock argues Dravidian specifically should not be credited as the significant source. Source: Substratum in Vedic Sanskrit, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-CON-12 (K) — A separate, Central Asian substrate associated with the BMAC contributed a recognisable loanword set shared by Sanskrit and Iranian (canal, brick, camel, hemp, mustard, ploughshare, needle, and others, about 55 words in Lubotsky's comparison); the prevailing interpretation is that this BMAC language was not Harappan, and its words entered Indo-Iranian before or during contact further north. Source: Substratum in Vedic Sanskrit, Wikipedia, opened in full, reporting Witzel, Lubotsky and Francfort & Tremblay (reference).
- IND-CON-13 (H) — Parpola's Dravidian reading of Meluhha itself — from Dravidian *mel* "elevated" + *akam* "place," with Brahui in Balochistan as a surviving Harappan remnant and Sanskrit *mleccha* "barbarian" as a derivative — identifies the Meluhhans as Proto-Dravidian speakers. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full, reporting Parpola, *Deciphering the Indus Script* (reference).
- IND-CON-14 (H) — The sesame word pair — Sumerian *ilu* / Akkadian *ellu* for sesame oil, supposedly exported by Harappans — derives from Dravidian *el* / *ellu* "sesame" (a Dravidian-contact argument), against Witzel's alternative derivation from a Para-Munda word for wild sesame, *jar-tila*; the same datum is claimed by both camps. Source: Meluhha, Wikipedia, opened in full (reference).
- IND-CON-15 (K) — Multiple languages were plausibly spoken within the Indus world, as Sumerian and Akkadian coexisted in Mesopotamia; McIntosh's specific version — Para-Munda dominant in the Punjab, Proto-Dravidian introduced by settlers into the southern plains in the 5th millennium BCE — is a hypothesis built on that plausibility. Source: Harappan language, Wikipedia (reference, reporting Jane R. McIntosh).

## The most consequential (H) axioms

These are the hypotheses whose variation would move an ensemble of ELSEWHERE world-model instances most. They are not the language-family question itself, which belongs to the language program's claims.

1. **Political unity vs. city-states vs. no state (IND-POL-08 / IND-POL-09 / IND-POL-10).** A single empire, a league of city-states, and a stateless merchant-organised society generate entirely different administrations, tax and sealing regimes, responses to drought, and reasons for simultaneous standardisation and simultaneous collapse. Every other domain's instances inherit this choice.
2. **The Ghaggar-Hakra's period flow (IND-ENV-06 vs. IND-ENV-07).** A monsoon-fed seasonal river that was already diminished in mature Harappan times (Singh / Clift) versus a perennial, Sutlej-fed river lasting to about 4.5 ka (Chatterjee et al.) changes where the eastern population lived, how its farming worked, how dense the Cholistan / Haryana settlement really was at any one moment (see IND-ENV-10), and whether river loss can bear causal weight in de-urbanisation.
3. **Meluhhan diaspora scale (IND-MOV-10, with IND-MOV-04 to IND-MOV-07).** A resident Indus colony at Guabba with its own granary, boats and an official interpreter implies sustained bilingual households, mixed families, and a plausible channel for names and words to cross — the very channel the program's Meluhha proper-name wedge needs. A thin, seasonal, broker-mediated contact (twenty seals, fourteen weights) implies the opposite: goods moved, people mostly did not, and external anchors will stay scarce.

**Runner-up, noted for completeness:** whether I6113 represents the whole civilisation (IND-DNA-12). If Harappan cities were genetically heterogeneous — as their isotopic migrant evidence hints — single-genome instances will systematically understate population diversity.

## Domains too thin to census honestly

- **No domain was left empty, but two are thin in kind, not just in count:**
  - **DNA (Section 4)** is thin by sample size, and the census says so in the axioms themselves: one published Harappan-site genome (IND-DNA-01), obtained from 1 of 61 attempted samples (IND-DNA-02), with the civilisation-wide picture resting on 11 outlier individuals from two non-Indus sites (IND-DNA-05). The axioms are solid as statements about the papers; they are a narrow base for statements about a civilisation.
  - **MOV, for movement *inside* the Harappan world,** is thin: this pass found isotopic migrant evidence (IND-MOV-09) and external-contact evidence in quantity, but no opened source that quantifies internal migration, marriage networks, or rural-to-urban movement. A census of internal mobility cannot honestly be built from what was opened here and should be marked as a gap, not filled.
- **POL is thin in a different way:** the excavated facts are firm (IND-POL-01) but every positive model of Harappan government is (H). The census can state what was *not* there with more confidence than what was.
