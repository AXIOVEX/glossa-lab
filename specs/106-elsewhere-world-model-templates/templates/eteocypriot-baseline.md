## Domain 2 — Geography (GEO)

- ETE-GEO-01 (K) — Cyprus lies in the eastern Mediterranean at a maritime crossroads connecting the Aegean, Anatolia, Egypt and the Levant, and its copper made it an essential node in Bronze Age and Iron Age exchange networks. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-GEO-02 (K) — Amathus stood on a small coastal hill about 10 km east of modern Limassol on the south coast, its acropolis hill 88 m high, sloping down to the sea, with cemeteries to the east, north and west. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Hermary).
- ETE-GEO-03 (K) — The Amathus acropolis carried a sanctuary of Aphrodite on its summit and a palace of the kings on the slope below it, the lower city lay between the foot of the hill and the sea, and its built harbour lay offshore. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-GEO-04 (K) — The kingdom of Amathus was small, with cereals, trees and vines on its coastal plain and copper and building timber in the part of the Troodos under its control, and water was its main difficulty, collected in cisterns. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Hermary).
- ETE-GEO-05 (K) — Amathus controlled the area east of the Kouris river, Kourion lay west of that river, and the kingdom of Kition lay beyond Amathus to the east. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Iacovou).
- ETE-GEO-06 (K) — Esarhaddon's list of 673–672 BCE names ten Cypriot polities: Idalion, Chytri, Salamis, Paphos, Soli, Kourion, Tamassos, Kition (as Qarti-hadasti, "New Town"), Ledra and Marion, and it omits Amathus and Lapithos, which are identified as additional autonomous centres. Source: Wikipedia, "Ten city-kingdoms of Cyprus" (reference, citing Iacovou 2018 and Radner 2025).
- ETE-GEO-07 (K) — Further Cypriot kingdoms named in the record beyond Esarhaddon's ten include Amathus, Lapithos (Lapathus) and Golgi (Golgoi), the last a sanctuary site inland, near modern Athienou, that produced non-Greek syllabic inscriptions. Source: Wikipedia, "Ten city-kingdoms of Cyprus" (reference); Wikipedia, "Eteocypriot language" (reference, on Golgoi/Athienou).
- ETE-GEO-08 (K) — The copper ore of Cyprus lies in a ring in the Troodos foothills, in massive sulphide bodies in the Pillow Lavas around the massif, and most Iron Age capitals lay within about 15 km of copper mines, Salamis, Ledra and Chytroi being the exceptions. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "A Kingdom for Fifty Talents: Copper and the City-Kingdoms of Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Iacovou and Kassianidou; read in search-result text).
- ETE-GEO-09 (K) — Kition, modern Larnaca, is on the south-east coast and was an important harbour town already in the Late Bronze Age. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-GEO-10 (K) — Tamassos was landlocked, had no harbour of its own, and would have needed another kingdom's port, in practice Kition's, to export its copper production. Source: Pestarino 2020, "A Cypriot city-kingdom for sale", Kadmos 59 (scholarly article; abstract read in search-result text).
- ETE-GEO-11 (H) — The Amathus acropolis, protected by limestone cliffs on three sides and looking west over the bay of Limassol to the Akrotiri peninsula, was intervisible with the coastwise sea route along the south coast, so shipping passing Amathus could be watched from the sanctuary hill. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis; the viewpoint is sourced, the intervisibility inference is this census's).

## Domain 3 — Politics and administration (POL)

- ETE-POL-01 (K) — Iron Age Cyprus was divided into autonomous small kingdoms, each ruled from a city by a king called basileus in Greek (mlk in Phoenician), and the number of kingdoms fluctuated, from ten named in 673/2 BCE to seven facing Alexander and Ptolemy in the late 4th century: Salamis, Kition, Amathus, Paphos, Marion, Soloi and Lapithos. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Cypro-Classical Cyprus" and "The Cypro-Archaic Period" (scholarly syntheses, citing Iacovou; read in search-result text).
- ETE-POL-02 (K) — There was no citizen polis and no democracy in these kingdoms; each struck its own coinage and kept its own cults. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Cypro-Classical Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis; read in search-result text).
- ETE-POL-03 (K) — Pseudo-Skylax and Theopompos called the Amathusians autochthonous, an old island people, and Amathus is consistently treated in the scholarship as the autochthonous kingdom, distinct from Greek Salamis and Phoenician Kition. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" and "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly syntheses, the latter citing Steele on Pseudo-Skylax).
- ETE-POL-04 (F) — In Herodotus's account, when Onesilos of Salamis persuaded all Cyprus to revolt from Persia during the Ionian Revolt, the Amathusians alone would not consent, and Onesilos besieged Amathus. Source: Herodotus, Histories 5.104, LacusCurtius (Loeb translation) (primary).
- ETE-POL-05 (F) — In Herodotus's account the Cypriot revolt was defeated in 498 BCE, Onesilos was killed, and the Amathusians hung his head over their gate, later taking it down and honouring him as a hero on an oracle's instruction after bees nested in the skull. Source: Herodotus, Histories 5.108–115 as summarised in Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Achaemenid Persia" and "The Cypro-Archaic Period" (primary text mediated by scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-POL-06 (K) — Under Persia, roughly from the 540s or 520s BCE until 332 BCE, no satrap was posted to Cyprus and no Persian official is attested there; the city-kings kept their thrones, cults, laws and coinage, and owed tribute and ships when the Great King went to war. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Achaemenid Persia" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-POL-07 (K) — Herodotus places Cyprus in Darius's fifth tribute district with all Phoenicia and Syria-Palestine, assessed together at 350 talents a year, and how much any one Cypriot kingdom paid is unknown. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "What Is Tribute?" and "Achaemenid Persia" (scholarly syntheses, citing Herodotus 3.91).
- ETE-POL-08 (K) — Before Persia, the Cypriot kingdoms stood in client relations first to Assyria (Sargon II's stele at Kition, c. 707 BCE, speaks of seven kings of Cyprus; Assyrian control is conventionally given as 707–612 BCE) and then to Egypt (570–525 BCE). Source: Wikipedia, "Ten city-kingdoms of Cyprus" (reference).
- ETE-POL-09 (K) — The Cypriot syllabary was the kingdoms' shared writing technology: it wrote both Greek and Eteocypriot, was used for royal inscriptions, dedications, epitaphs and coin legends, and persisted beside the Phoenician alphabet (from the 9th century) and the Greek alphabet (from the Classical period). Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Steele).
- ETE-POL-10 (K) — Many kingdoms began minting their own coinage by the late 6th century BCE; coinage is attested with certainty for Salamis, Kition, Amathus, Paphos, Marion, Soloi and Lapithos, with legends in the Cypriot syllabary, in Phoenician, and from the 4th century also in Greek. Source: Wikipedia, "Ten city-kingdoms of Cyprus" (reference); Cyprus Numismatic Project / Kyprios Character project page, University of Edinburgh (reference; read in search-result text).
- ETE-POL-11 (K) — The kingdom system ended politically in the late 4th century: Alexander took Cyprus in 333 BCE, Androkles, the last king of Amathus, sided with Alexander, and the Ptolemies abolished the kingdoms between about 312 and 310/294 BCE. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis); Wikipedia, "Ten city-kingdoms of Cyprus" (reference).
- ETE-POL-12 (K) — Kingdoms could be absorbed or sold: Kition took over inland Idalion and Tamassos, and Tamassos's last king Pasikypros sold his kingdom to Pumayyaton of Kition for fifty talents and retired to Amathus. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "A Kingdom for Fifty Talents" (scholarly synthesis, citing Duris of Samos via Athenaeus; read in search-result text).

## Domain 4 — People movement (MOV)

- ETE-MOV-01 (K) — Phoenicians, likely from Tyre, established a permanent presence at Kition around the 9th century BCE, rebuilding a large temple, possibly to Astarte, on Late Bronze Age foundations, and the first confirmed Phoenician inscriptions in Cyprus date to the 9th century and are associated with that settlement. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-MOV-02 (K) — Kition's Phoenicianisation was gradual: Phoenician wares increase markedly toward the end of the 8th century BCE, which may mark the transition from a Cypriot city with resident Phoenician traders to a fully Phoenician-administered polity, a Tyrian dependency. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-MOV-03 (K) — At Amathus, by contrast, Phoenician ceramics indicate extensive trade rather than a distinct Phoenician residential quarter, so the Phoenician presence there was commercial, not a settlement colony on the Kition model. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-MOV-04 (K) — The Greek dialect of Cyprus, Cypriot Greek, is the close relative of Arcadian in the Peloponnese, and the pair, Arcado-Cypriot, is the historic Greek dialect group closest to the Mycenaean Greek of Linear B, a likeness explained by common descent from a Mycenaean-period dialect. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Iacovou and Morpurgo Davies).
- ETE-MOV-05 (K) — An 11th-century BCE bronze spit (obelos) from Palaepaphos-Skales bears the Greek name Opheltas in syllabic signs, the earliest Greek text on Cyprus, and Iacovou reads it as a marker for the arrival of Greek speakers of the Arcado-Cypriot group and their adoption of the syllabary by about 1000 BCE, a reading other scholars contest. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-MOV-06 (K) — Greek foundation traditions tied Cypriot cities to heroes returning from Troy, Pausanias reporting that Agapenor the Arcadian founded Paphos and built the sanctuary of Aphrodite Paphia, and such traditions are treated as reflecting, at most, the outlines of Late Bronze Age settlement. Source: Wikipedia, "Arcadocypriot Greek" (reference, citing Pausanias; read in search-result text); Wikipedia, "Ten city-kingdoms of Cyprus" (reference).
- ETE-MOV-07 (K) — Trade ran in both directions: Tyre, Dor and Sidon have produced large quantities of Cypriot pottery, indicating a substantial Cypriot presence and Cypriot traders in Levantine ports, not only Phoenicians in Cyprus. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-MOV-08 (K) — Cypriots served abroad and wrote abroad: two Eteocypriot graffiti appear among Greek syllabic graffiti at Abydos in Egypt, and Cypriots are attested as sailors and mercenaries, including the kittiyim of the Arad ostraca in Judah. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Steele); Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Cypro-Archaic Period" (scholarly synthesis; read in search-result text).
- ETE-MOV-09 (H) — Bilingual households and routine intermarriage between Greek-, Phoenician- and Eteocypriot-speaking Cypriots are plausible given the shared material culture and bilingual public inscriptions, but no source opened in this pass directly documents intermarriage or household language use, so this stays a hypothesis. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis, on shared material culture and hybridisation; the household claim itself is UNSOURCED in the opened record).

## Domain 5 — DNA and population (DNA)

**This domain is thin, and the census says so plainly: there is effectively no published ancient-DNA sampling of Iron Age Cypriots, and none at all of identified Eteocypriot speakers. The axioms below are mostly statements about that absence and about neighbouring evidence that must not be over-read as Cypriot Iron Age evidence.**

- ETE-DNA-01 (K) — The large Southern Arc study, Lazaridis et al. 2022, sequenced 727 ancient individuals from Anatolia and its neighbours in south-eastern Europe and West Asia over about 11,000 years, but its focus is the Chalcolithic and Bronze Ages, about 5000 to 1000 BCE, not Iron Age Cyprus. Source: Lazaridis et al., "The genetic history of the Southern Arc", Science 377 (2022) (scholarly, primary paper PDF opened).
- ETE-DNA-02 (K) — In that study's framing, Anatolia was transformed by intra-West Asian gene flow with negligible impact from the later Yamnaya migrations, while steppe ancestry spread south into the Balkans and across the Caucasus into Armenia. Source: Lazaridis et al., "The genetic history of the Southern Arc", Science 377 (2022) (scholarly).
- ETE-DNA-03 (K) — No individual identified as Eteocypriot has been genetically tested, and the genetic identity of the Eteocypriots remains speculative in the secondary literature that discusses it. Source: secondary discussion of Cypriot genetics summarising Voskarides et al. 2016 and Heraclides et al. 2016 (reference-level; read in search-result text).
- ETE-DNA-04 (H) — Any claim that Iron Age Amathusians were genetically distinct from, or identical to, Iron Age Salaminians or Kitians is at present untestable from published data, because the direct Iron Age Cypriot sample does not exist in the opened record. Source: Lazaridis et al. 2022 (scholarly; the absence is this census's finding from the opened record) — flagged as an absence claim, revisable on any new publication.
- ETE-DNA-05 (H) UNSOURCED — Modern Cypriot haplogroup distributions are sometimes used to argue a substantial prehistoric, possibly Eteocypriot, contribution to the island's gene pool, but no source opened in this pass supports projecting modern distributions back onto Iron Age Amathus, and an ELSEWHERE instance must not do so.

## Domain 6 — Material economy (ECO)

- ETE-ECO-01 (K) — Copper was what Cyprus had to sell: it was the island's defining export from the Bronze Age into the Iron Age, and Iacovou's dominant framework holds that the spread of the ore field around Troodos shaped the island's division into many small kingdoms, each needing a mining hinterland, a coastal outlet and farmland to feed its people. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "A Kingdom for Fifty Talents" (scholarly synthesis, citing Iacovou and Kassianidou; read in search-result text).
- ETE-ECO-02 (K) — Iron Age copper working was continuous, not a Phoenician restart: radiocarbon dates from mining spoil and timbers near Tamassos (Agrokipia-Kriadhis, Kambia) and at Skouriotissa in Soloi's territory fall in the 10th to 8th centuries BCE. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "A Kingdom for Fifty Talents" (scholarly synthesis, citing Kassianidou; read in search-result text).
- ETE-ECO-03 (K) — Tamassos is the only Cypriot kingdom ancient writers credit with great copper mines, Strabo writing that at Tamassos there are abundant mines of copper, and its mines were the stake in its sale to Kition. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "A Kingdom for Fifty Talents" (scholarly synthesis, citing Strabo; read in search-result text).
- ETE-ECO-04 (K) — After about 1100 BCE the oxhide ingot, the diagnostic Bronze Age form of Cypriot copper abroad, goes out of use, and shipped Iron Age Cypriot copper is archaeologically hard to trace, so Iron Age export volumes cannot be read off ingot finds. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "A Kingdom for Fifty Talents" (scholarly synthesis, citing Kassianidou; read in search-result text).
- ETE-ECO-05 (K) — Amathus's own resources were agricultural and extractive together: cereals, trees and vines on the coastal plain, and copper and building timber in its Troodos territory. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Hermary).
- ETE-ECO-06 (K) — Amathus's built harbour, about 100 by 180 m with offshore moles still visible, was built only at the very end of the 4th century BCE and was soon left unused, so for most of the Eteocypriot period Amathus traded without that built harbour. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-ECO-07 (K) — Cypriot kingdoms exported beyond copper: the island also sent out gold, textiles, timber and grain in the Archaic period. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Cypro-Archaic Period" (scholarly synthesis; read in search-result text).
- ETE-ECO-08 (K) — Trade goods moved both ways at scale: Cypriot pottery is abundant at Tyre, Dor and Sidon, Phoenician wares including Black-on-Red (Cypro-Phoenician) juglets circulated widely and were made in large numbers on Cyprus by the end of the Cypro-Geometric period, and imported luxury goods in gold, silver and ivory reached Cypriot centres. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-ECO-09 (K) — Coinage, once kingdoms began minting by the late 6th century BCE, was struck on differing weight standards by different kings, and coins carry much of the surviving king-list evidence, at Paphos ten of eighteen known kings surviving only through their coins. Source: Cyprus Numismatic Project / Kyprios Character project page (reference; read in search-result text); Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Cypro-Classical Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis; read in search-result text).
- ETE-ECO-10 (K) — Amathus's palace storerooms, about 400 square metres holding large storage jars, with syllabic signs on jar fragments probably as identification or inventory marks, show the kingdom's administration physically storing and marking foodstuffs on the acropolis. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Hermary).

## Domain 7 — Environment and chronology (ENV)

- ETE-ENV-01 (K) — The conventional Cypriot Iron Age periodisation used in the scholarship opened here is Cypro-Geometric c. 1050–750 BCE, Cypro-Archaic c. 750–480 BCE and Cypro-Classical c. 480–310 BCE. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Society in Ancient Cyprus" document summary and "The Phoenicians" (scholarly syntheses).
- ETE-ENV-02 (K) — The Eteocypriot corpus, defined by recurring non-Greek features, comprises about 20 to 30 inscriptions (22 to 25 in another count), most from Amathus, running from the 8th or 7th century to the late 4th century BCE. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Steele); Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Cypro-Geometric: The Start of the Iron Age" (scholarly synthesis; read in search-result text).
- ETE-ENV-03 (K) — ICS 196, the Amathus bilingual, is a black marble slab from the Amathus acropolis, found about 1913 and since lost, dated to the late fourth century BCE, with the Eteocypriot text in the syllabary above a shorter Greek text in the alphabet. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Steele); Steele 2013, A Linguistic History of Ancient Cyprus, frontmatter concordance EC 1 = ICS 196, "late fourth century" (scholarly).
- ETE-ENV-04 (K) — The Glossa-Lab program's frozen census of ICS 196 is 11 segments and 54 signs (51 legible, 1 restored, 2 damaged), and the program held the text from secondary reproductions, not from Masson's edition of record (ICS pp. 206–209) or the Sittig 1914 photograph. Source: Glossa-Lab program record, SCOPE-eteocypriot.md and gap-analysis-2026-11-25.md (program records).
- ETE-ENV-05 (K) — Writing on Cyprus begins with Cypro-Minoan in the Late Bronze Age, from about 1600 BCE at Enkomi, a corpus of roughly 200 to 250 inscriptions and fewer than 3,000 signs, still undeciphered, morphologically closest to Minoan Linear A. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Steele and Hirschfeld).
- ETE-ENV-06 (K) — The developed Cypriot Syllabary appears with certainty in 8th-century inscriptions, had about 55 signs, each an open syllable, and survived in use into the early 2nd century BCE at a rural sanctuary near Nicosia (Kafizin). Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Steele).
- ETE-ENV-07 (K) — Amathus itself was founded on an empty site late in the 11th century BCE, with no Bronze Age town beneath it, unlike Salamis, Paphos or Kition. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Hermary and Iacovou).
- ETE-ENV-08 (K) — Modern Cyprus is semi-arid, the Larnaca Salt Lake area receiving about 351.5 mm of rain a year, and multi-year droughts are historically documented to have had devastating effects on Cypriot agriculture. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Climate and Landscape in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Kearns, Meyer and Knapp).
- ETE-ENV-09 (H) — A roughly 300-year drought centred on c. 1200 to 850 BCE, inferred mainly from the Larnaca Salt Lake pollen core, has been proposed as a stressor at the Bronze Age to Iron Age transition, but its dating rests on interpolation from three radiocarbon dates, none inside the drought window, and specialists treat the case as provisional. Source: Kaniewski et al. 2013, PLoS ONE (scholarly; read in search-result text); Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Climate and Landscape in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Knapp and Manning's critique).
- ETE-ENV-10 (K) — For the Iron Age proper, c. 8th to 4th centuries BCE, Cyprus has no long, well-dated local climate record: Kearns notes a general absence of absolutely dated Iron Age remains for environmental reconstruction, and archaeologists have leaned on archives from elsewhere in the Mediterranean, so no fine-grained Iron Age Cypriot climate claim is honestly available. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Climate and Landscape in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Kearns).

## Domain 8 — Language contact (CON)

- ETE-CON-01 (K) — Three main languages were in use in first-millennium Cyprus: Phoenician, a Semitic language written in the Phoenician alphabet; the Arcado-Cypriot Greek dialect, written in the Cypriot syllabary; and Eteocypriot, an undeciphered non-Greek language also written in the syllabary. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-CON-02 (F) — Eteocypriot is written in the Cypriot syllabary, whose sign values are known from its use for Greek, so Eteocypriot texts can be transliterated sign by sign even though the language is not understood. Source: Glossa-Lab program record, gap-analysis-2026-11-25.md (program record, stated as background); Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-CON-03 (K) — The Amathus bilingual, ICS 196, is the sole complete Eteocypriot bilingual: its Greek text reads "The city of the Amathusians [honoured] Ariston, son of Aristonax, of noble birth", the Eteocypriot text gives the same names with non-Greek endings and is roughly twice as long, so the two are broadly corresponding versions, not an exact translation. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Steele); Woudhuizen, "The Amathus Bilingual Inscription" (scholarly article; read in search-result text).
- ETE-CON-04 (K) — Three further fragmentary bilinguals from Amathus include a statue dedication for the son of King Androkles and a dedication to Cypriot Aphrodite naming the same king, and Androkles's own dedications show Amathus using alphabetic Greek beside the syllabary at the end of the 4th century. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" and "Amathus" (scholarly syntheses).
- ETE-CON-05 (K) — Phoenician-Greek bilinguals also exist: the Tamassos bilinguals (ICS 265 and ICS 215) are dedications in Phoenician and Cypriot-syllabic Greek dated by the regnal years of Milkyaton, king of Kition, to 375 and 362 BCE, and the Idalion bilingual (ICS 220) gives the same text in Greek and Phoenician. Source: Pestarino 2020, Kadmos 59 (scholarly article; read in search-result text); Davis 2010, "Introduction to Aegean Pre-Alphabetic Scripts" (scholarly; read in search-result text).
- ETE-CON-06 (K) — Script use was regionally divided and identity-bearing: at Kition inscriptions are almost exclusively Phoenician from the 9th to the 4th centuries BCE, while the Phoenician alphabet is almost entirely absent at Paphos, where the Greek syllabary was used from the 10th century BCE. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "The Phoenicians" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-CON-07 (K) — Securely identified Eteocypriot is confined in the record to Amathus and a few short inscriptions from Golgoi, and Egetmeyer has suggested the Golgoi texts, which he calls Golgian, may be a different language from Amathusian Eteocypriot. Source: Wikipedia, "Eteocypriot language" (reference, citing Egetmeyer 2010).
- ETE-CON-08 (K) — Eteocypriot was under pressure from Arcadocypriot Greek from about the 10th century BCE, was used at Amathus at least until the time of Alexander the Great, and became extinct in about the 4th century BCE. Source: Wikipedia, "Eteocypriot language" (reference); Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Amathus" (scholarly synthesis).
- ETE-CON-09 (K) — What can be recovered of Eteocypriot is a set of noun endings (-o-se, -i, -o, -o-ti), a handful of recurrent words such as a-na, and a phonology that kept /w/ and /y/ stable, and none of this matches a known language family. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Steele).
- ETE-CON-10 (H) — Thierry Petit (1999) proposed that Eteocypriot is a Hurrian dialect, and further that it was not the first spoken language in Cyprus. Source: Petit 1999, "Eteocypriot myth and Amathusian reality", Journal of Mediterranean Archaeology 12(1), as cited in Wikipedia, "Eteocypriot language" (reference citing scholarly article).

## The most consequential (H) axioms

1. **ETE-CON-13 (H) — Substrate survival vs later arrival.** The usual assumption is that Eteocypriot continued a language spoken on Cyprus in the second millennium BCE, with Cypro-Minoan sign-groups ending in sign 023 (cognate with ti) as the best hint of a link to the Eteocypriot -o-ti ending; but the identity, or even ancestry, of the Cypro-Minoan language(s) and Eteocypriot is unproven, and Petit's Hurrian proposal (ETE-CON-10) implies the opposite, a later arrival that was not the island's first language. Source: Ancient Cyprus compendium, "Writing and Literacy in Ancient Cyprus" (scholarly synthesis, citing Steele); Wikipedia, "Eteocypriot language" (reference). *Why consequential: an ELSEWHERE instance spawned on the substrate reading models Amathus as a refuge of a Bronze Age population; on the arrival reading it models a migrant community. Almost every MOV and DNA prior flips with this choice.*
2. **ETE-CON-14 (H) UNSOURCED — Social domain of the language.** Whether Eteocypriot in the 5th–4th centuries was still the full community language of Amathus, or had narrowed to a prestige, cultic and honorific register used on public stone while Greek was spoken at home, is not settled by any source opened in this pass. The bilingual habit itself (Eteocypriot first and longer, Greek below, ETE-CON-03) is compatible with both. An ELSEWHERE instance must declare which it assumes.
3. **ETE-MOV-09 (H), restated — Bilingual households.** Routine intermarriage and bilingual households would make language contact a domestic, not merely monumental, phenomenon; the opened record supports hybrid material culture and bilingual public texts but does not document households. The instance's contact intensity depends on this unproven axiom.

## Domains too thin to census honestly

- **DNA (Section 4) is too thin to census as a domain.** There is, in the opened record, no published ancient-DNA sample from Iron Age Cyprus and no tested Eteocypriot individual. The five lines above are therefore mostly absence statements and guardrails. An ELSEWHERE instance spawned from this census must carry DNA as an open variable, not as a prior.
- **ENV, for the Iron Age proper, is thin at fine resolution.** Periodisation, corpus dates and the semi-arid baseline are solid (K); any year-by-year or century-by-century Iron Age Cypriot climate narrative is not (ETE-ENV-09, ETE-ENV-10). Climate should enter an instance only as semi-arid marginality plus drought risk, never as a dated event inside the frame period.
- **Household-level language contact (within CON/MOV) is thin.** Public, monumental bilingualism is well attested; private and domestic language use is not attested in the opened record at all.
